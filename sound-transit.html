Information Architecture Redesign · UW iSchool · 2026
Aashika Ashok, Amy Lee, Catherine Pan, Jane Guo

soundtransit.org ↗

Introduction

Sound Transit runs Link light rail, Sounder trains, and ST Express buses across the Seattle region — moving hundreds of thousands of people every day. But soundtransit.org wasn't built around any of them. It was built around departments.

For our UW iSchool 2026 capstone, our team — Aashika, Catherine, Jane, and me — partnered with Sound Transit to rethink the site's information architecture. Not a visual refresh. A structural one. We mapped five real journeys — a first-time rider, an airport arrival, a daily commuter, a game-night fan, a contractor and a neighbor — and redesigned the site so each person could find what they needed without already knowing how transit websites work.

The first ride shouldn't require a scavenger hunt

Picture someone who just moved to Seattle. They want to take light rail for the first time — but the basics are scattered. How to plan a trip lives on one page. ORCA card info on another. Where and how to tap? Good luck finding it. Fares, arguably the first thing a new rider needs, had no clear home in the navigation.

We built a Rider Guide that walks someone through the whole journey in order: plan your trip, get an ORCA card, tap on and off. Fares earned its own place in the nav — because if you can't figure out what it costs, you probably won't ride.

Land at SeaTac. Train in minutes.

A traveler steps off a long flight at SeaTac. They have luggage, no ORCA card, and one question: when is the next train? The old airport page buried that answer under general transit information — the same layout you'd use for someone planning a trip next week.

We redesigned the SeaTac page for urgency. Next train up top. Popular destinations one tap away. Quick links to buy a ticket or find the station. A "know before you travel" section for the things that actually trip people up — where to tap, how long it takes, what to do with bags.

The commute check that couldn't wait

Every morning, the same ritual: open the site, check if your line is running, decide whether to leave now or wait. For daily commuters, service alerts aren't a nice-to-have — they're the reason you trust the system or start driving.

On the current site, alerts live deep in the hierarchy — easy to miss when you're in a hurry. We pulled Alerts into its own dedicated navigation section, so the first thing a commuter sees is exactly what they came for: is my train on time?

Mariners game tonight. Last train when?

It's a Friday. You're heading to T-Mobile Park for a Mariners game. You need to know which station to use, how long the walk is, when the last train leaves, and whether there's extended service after the final out. That's four separate questions — and on the old site, four separate searches.

We designed a Games and Events experience that puts tonight's game front and center: station options with walk times, train schedules, and extended service details — all reachable in one tap. Because missing the last train home shouldn't be part of the ballpark experience.

Two visitors, one agency

Not everyone on soundtransit.org is catching a train. A contractor needs to find open bids and figure out how to do business with the agency. A community member wants to attend a public meeting or join the Sounding Board. Both are legitimate users — and both were getting lost in pages built for someone else.

We consolidated contracting under Doing Business With Us — one front door for vendors and partners. Community Participation got its own home, with the Sounding Board and Event Calendar surfaced together. Same agency, different needs — each with a clear path in.

Built around the people it serves

Sound Transit connects the region — light rail from the airport to downtown, buses across counties, Sounder up and down the corridor. The website should do the same kind of work: get riders where they're going, help contractors find opportunities, give neighbors a seat at the table, and welcome first-time visitors without making them feel like outsiders.

We reorganized soundtransit.org around the people who actually use it — not the departments that built it.
