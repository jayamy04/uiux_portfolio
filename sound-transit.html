Background

We are conducting user interviews to better understand how riders navigate the Sound Transit website, what information they prioritize, and where they experience confusion when navigating the website.

Our prior research methods have provided strong behavioral and structural insights:

  • GA4 analysis revealed common user paths and drop-off points
  • Heuristic evaluation identified usability issues
  • Content audit clarified key user groups and their Jobs-to-Be-Done (JTBD)
  • Competitive analysis highlighted how similar transit systems organize content
  • Card sorting revealed users' mental models for grouping information

However, these methods do not fully explain why users behave the way they do or how they interpret the current information architecture. User interviews will provide deeper insight into users' mental models, expectations, and pain points which will help us redesign the IA.

Research Goals

  • Understand how users conceptualize and navigate transit-related information
  • Identify mismatches between user mental models and the current IA
  • Discover key pain points, confusion points, and navigation breakdowns
  • Understand what information users prioritize and expect to find easily
  • Learn how users approach common tasks (e.g., trip planning, fares, service updates)
  • Validate and contextualize findings from GA4, card sorting, and heuristic evaluation

Research Questions

  1. 01

    What are the most common tasks users come to the website to complete, and what information are they looking for?

  2. 02

    Which tasks are easiest for users to complete, and which are the most difficult? How do users currently work around any challenges?

  3. 03

    Where do users experience the most confusion or friction, and where do they experience the least?

  4. 04

    What terminology or labels are most clear to users, and which ones cause the most confusion?

  5. 05

    What aspects of the current site structure are working well, if any?

Findings

We tested a mid-fi prototype of the redesigned IA across rider and community/business sessions.

All three participants found the top-level separation between Riders, Projects, and About directionally correct — the structural decisions held. The failures were label-level and interaction-level.

Emergency contacts

All three participants failed to find emergency contact information. The "?" button was interpreted as a chatbot by all three. P3 said she "would not go there if it was urgent." This is a safety-critical failure, not just a UX one.

Contact Us

All three participants looked for contact in the footer or utility nav first. P3 said she would look for the footer of the site before anywhere else → Customer Service & Contact surfaced under Help in the final IA.

Fares

P2 said getting an ORCA card is a different task from seeing fare prices.

"Get Involved" label

P2 said it was muddy and unclear, and suggested renaming to Work With Us. P3 independently said the same thing — that it wasn't clear both business and rider users could "Get Involved," and suggested Work With Us as a rename. Two participants landing on the same rename unprompted was a strong signal.

Blog and News placement

All three participants independently said Blog and News belong under About, not Get Involved. P2 said News in Get Involved felt like it was for collaborators, not the public. P3 said if the content is company updates, it should be in About.

Community Participation label

P1 said Community Events sounded like rider entertainment — similar to Games and Events for Riders — and couldn't distinguish civic participation from event entertainment based on the label alone. This matched tree testing, where 16 out of 24 participants could not find it.

Jobs

P3 said Jobs feels more like it belongs under About.

Overview

Sound Transit is delivering the largest transit expansion program in the nation, with voter-approved projects from 1996, 2008, and 2016 adding new Light Rail, Bus, and Commuter Rail service across the Puget Sound region. As our system grows, so do the needs of our riders, partners, and community members who rely on soundtransit.org for everything from trip planning, to business opportunities, to project transparency. The digital team invites a team of HCDE students to inform improvements to the user experience of Sound Transit's website by researching and proposing a re-design for its navigation and information architecture.

Over the course of the capstone project, students should analyze the current website's taxonomy and user flows, conduct user research and data analysis to define personas and audience segments, and propose a new user-centered site architecture that enhances discoverability, efficiency, and accessibility. A key goal is to ensure that the redesigned structure is adaptable for future needs and scalable design systems.

The resulting recommendations and prototypes will inform Sound Transit's next-generation digital experience.

Background

As Sound Transit grows, so do the information needs of the public, who range in audience type from passengers to business partners, to community members. ST has considered opportunities to improve the experience for users of soundtransit.org through a site refresh, including how to streamline the experience for the varied audiences that we serve. While discussions have taken place regarding the re-architecting of specific sections of the website, Sound Transit has not looked holistically at the discoverability of content under the existing navigation structure.

By partnering with a capstone team at UW HCDE, ST aims to gather data to inform a re-design effort and receive recommendations on how to modernize

Goals

  1. Goal 1

    Assess the discoverability of content under the existing IA

  2. Goal 2

    Understand how to better organize content to meet different audience needs, such as the UX of passengers, board members, partners, contractors and community members.

  3. Goal 3

    Inform the roadmap of a site-wide re-design

  4. Goal 4

    Develop user personas/ segments for soundtransit.org, and considerations for how to incorporate these personas into design practices

Scope

  1. Phase 1

    1) Phase 1: Project Plan: Students will work with the ST project team to develop a project plan for deliverables and schedule throughout the duration of the project. Students should develop an understanding of the current state practices of content production at Sound Transit and have a clear understanding of ST's goals for the capstone effort.

  2. Phase 2

    2) Phase 2: Research: Students should leverage best practices in UX research, data analysis, and information architecture to conduct a thorough audit of the existing site architecture. While the project deliverables will be specified in the project plan developed in phase 1, suggested research activities may include:

    • Content audit—inventory of all content/pages on the site, mapped to different audiences
    • Analysis of GA4 data
    • Best practices review with IA
    • Peer agency research
    • Persona/user segment development
    • Card sorting
    • Stakeholder interviews with the VEDD and Strategic Comms teams.
  3. Phase 3

    3) Phase 3: Design: Students should develop recommendations for how to improve site structure and navigation, based on the insights generated during the research phase. This may include the following:

    • Site architecture map
    • Revised taxonomy
    • Optional: few prototypes for new user experiences proposed
    • A roadmap of how to roll out incremental improvements
    • Iterations of deliverables, as tested and validated by stakeholder feedback and evaluative usability testing

Out of Scope

  • The work should be more conceptual design than visual design.
    • As such, an evaluation of the style guide and design system is out of scope. If necessary to convey recommended design changes that would follow a re-structuring, additional components can be proposed but should not be the focus of the re-design effort.
    • Similarly, the eventual verbiage used in any re-naming should be determined by the VEDD team. A proposed new taxonomy should use conceptual or descriptive labels, rather than take concern with the verbiage itself.

Assumptions

  • All deliverables received from the student team are considered as recommendations and do not commit Sound Transit to acting upon them.
  • The student team will have access to research tools to inform their work, including participant recruitment resources (UserLytics) and reports of Google Analytics data.
  • The project team should be available to provide guidance via a 30-60 minute meeting (weekly or bi-weekly). At the request of the student team, key stakeholder interviews or focus groups can be arranged with advanced notice.
  • The student team should be encouraged to think creatively, while understanding organizational needs and constraints. The partnership should be a collaborative partnership, rather than as a consulting agreement.

Who We Were Designing For

Before any research, we mapped four distinct rider audiences:

  • First-time riders figuring out how to ride before they leave home — how much it costs, whether they need a card, what to do when they board.
  • SeaTac travelers flying into Seattle who want light rail into the city. They need to know which line, how long it takes, and how to pay — often from a phone in an unfamiliar airport.
  • Frequent commuters who ride the 1 Line every weekday and need a fast check: is service running normally before they walk out the door.
  • Event riders heading to a Seahawks game who need to know which station to go to, whether there's extra service, and when the last train leaves.

Beyond riders, the site also serves businesses and contractors looking for procurement opportunities, and community members following expansion projects or wanting to attend public meetings. The current structure served none of these audiences clearly.

Research

We ran six research methods over the six-month engagement. Every structural recommendation in the final IA traces back to a specific finding from at least one of these methods — usually more than one.

GA4 Behavioral Analytics

We analyzed Sound Transit's Google Analytics data going back to when GA4 was first implemented on the site.

405,000 monthly active users. 618,000 sessions came from organic Google search versus 128,000 direct — meaning most people arriving on the site were looking for something specific and landing directly on a page, bypassing navigation entirely.

70% of users were on mobile. The site was built for desktop. GA4 path data showed looping behavior on mobile — users navigating forward, hitting a dead end, going back, and trying again — which we attributed to the absence of breadcrumbs and the lack of onward links from key pages.

Top internal search terms: Stride BRT (95 searches), map/Maps (66/65/39), parking (64/57), Fares (34), Cost (30), West Seattle Link (35), Ballard (27). High internal search volume for content that already exists on the site is a direct signal that users cannot find it through navigation. If the nav worked, they wouldn't be searching for it.

  • 405K Monthly active users
  • 70% Sessions on mobile
  • 618K Organic search sessions
  • 144K Rider-to-Maps navigations per month

Bounce Rate Analysis

Every single one of these pages had a 100% bounce rate:

  • soundtransit.org/ride-with-us/know-before-you-go/new-to-sound-transit
  • soundtransit.org/ride-with-us/how-to-pay/ticket-machines
  • soundtransit.org/ride-with-us/popular-destinations/mariners
  • soundtransit.org/ride-with-us/know-before-you-go/bringing-your-bike

A 100% bounce rate doesn't mean users got what they needed — it means there were no onward paths. Users arrived, failed to find what they needed, and left.

Card Sorting (56 participants)

We ran two card sort studies with 56 total participants, asking them to organize Sound Transit's content into groups that made sense to them.

"Ride With Us" and "Get to Know Us" — the current top-level nav labels — appeared zero times across all 56 participants as meaningful category labels. Nobody used these words when organizing the content. That was the single most damning finding of the entire study.

The strongest grouping: Trip planner, schedules, routes, and maps were consistently grouped together by both frequent riders and casual users. Maps lived under Get to Know Us on the current site — 144,000 users per month had to leave the Rider section to find it.

Alerts were universally placed as standalone content — not nested inside any other section — across both participant groups.

Heuristic Evaluation

We evaluated the current site against Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics. Severity 4 (critical) findings:

  • Service alerts are not accessible from primary navigation — users cannot find disruption information without already knowing where to look
  • Breadcrumbs are absent on mobile — users cannot orient themselves or navigate back without using the browser back button
  • ORCA is presented as a label without explanation — it assumes prior knowledge that many users, especially tourists and first-time riders, do not have

Competitive Analysis (8+ transit platforms)

Every peer agency — TransLink, BART, RTD Denver — treats alerts as high-priority surfaced content, leads payment pages with a card recommendation, and treats Help as secondary utility navigation rather than primary nav. Sound Transit's current structure did the opposite on all three.

Tree Testing (2 rounds, 24 total participants)

Round 1 tested the existing IA structure with 19 participants on both desktop and mobile — establishing a baseline for where the current navigation broke down.

Round 2 tested our redesigned IA with 5 participants on both desktop and mobile — validating label and structural changes before stakeholder handoff.

Key tree test findings that directly shaped the final IA:

  • 16 out of 24 participants could not find Community Engagement in tree testing → renamed to Community Participation
  • P1 and P4 navigated to Planning Future Service when looking for a public meeting → crosslink added from Projects to Community Participation
  • 20% of participants went to the 1 Line page rather than the Alerts section when checking for disruptions → Alerts surfaced inline on individual line pages in addition to standalone nav
  • Two independent participants across both rounds flagged ST Shop as confusing → kept as ST Shop in the final IA pending stakeholder decision on renaming
  • Participants consistently grouped Blog and News Releases together in card sorting, and Blog attracted wrong clicks for press releases and annual reports in tree testing → merged with News Releases

The Core Problem

The site was organized around how Sound Transit thinks about itself internally — not around how riders, businesses, and community members think about what they need. Five specific structural failures:

  • Navigation labels nobody recognized. Ride With Us and Get to Know Us appeared zero times across 56 card sort participants.
  • Critical content buried. Service alerts lived inside a subsection. Fares required three clicks. Maps was in the wrong section for 144,000 users a month.
  • Dead end pages. 100% bounce rate on all rider guide-adjacent pages with no onward paths.
  • 70% mobile, 0% mobile-first thinking. No breadcrumbs, broken icons, a map that trapped scroll events.
  • Four audiences, zero clear entry points. A first-time rider and a contractor arrive at the same homepage with no signal of where their path starts.

The Proposed IA

We restructured the site around three top-level tabs: Riders, Projects, and About.

Riders

Ride With Us — Trip Planner, Station Finder, SeaTac Airport, Riding to Games & Events, Popular Destinations, Accessibility, Parking.

Schedules & Maps — Service Map, Route Schedules. Moved out of Get to Know Us based on card sort data showing maps consistently grouped with planning content, and GA4 showing 144K users navigating there from Rider pages.

Fares — Surfaced as a standalone section. The single most-searched content category on the site.

Alerts — Elevated to standalone top-level section. Supported by card sort (universally standalone across 56 participants), heuristic evaluation (severity 4), and internal search data showing users were searching for Alerts because navigation wasn't surfacing it.

Rider Guide — Consolidated first-time rider content that previously existed across scattered dead-end pages. One sequential flow: Plan Your Trip → Get an ORCA Card → How to Ride.

Help — Customer Service & Contact, Lost & Found, Accessibility Inquiries, Translation Services, FAQs. Kept within Riders but with contact information surfaced prominently, addressing the usability test finding that all three participants expected to find contact info here.

Projects

Current Projects — active expansion and infrastructure work.

Future Plans & Initiatives — Planning Future Service, Building The System, Environment & Sustainability.

Spring/Summer Progress Report and Environment and Sustainability as standalone items.

About

Who We Are — Our History, Board of Directors, Office of Civil Rights Equity and Inclusion, Our Brand, Panels & Committees, Innovation & Technology.

Doing Business With Us — Contracting Opportunities, Procurement Contracts and Agreements, Contractor Resources, Project Leadership.

Community Participation — Sounding Board, Event Calendar. Renamed from Community Engagement — 16 out of 24 participants could not find it in tree testing under that label.

Jobs, News Releases, Documents, ST Shop as standalone items.
