Methods
Card sorting, heuristic evaluation, moderated usability testing, competitive analysis, GA4 behavioral analytics, tree testing
Duration

soundtransit.org ↗

The Problem

soundtransit.org is a critical public resource — riders rely on it to plan trips, find schedules, and navigate emergencies.

The problem wasn't missing content. It was that the structure didn't match how riders think about getting around.

What We Found

GA4 behavioral analytics gave us the scale of the problem:

  • 618K internal search sessions — the top terms were "Fares," "Cost," "Schedule," "Map," "Airport," "Tacoma Dome." All of this content existed on the site. Users were searching for it because navigation wasn't getting them there. "Ride with us" and "Get to know us" — the actual nav section names — appeared zero times across 56 card sort participants. Nobody thinks in those terms.
  • 144K users navigated from Rider pages to Maps — meaning Maps was a core rider need, but riders had to leave the Rider section entirely to find it.
  • 70% of users are on mobile, but the site was built for desktop. GA4 showed looping behavior — users navigating back and forth repeatedly — which matched the absence of breadcrumbs flagged in our heuristic evaluation.
  • Several pages had a 100% bounce rate: new-to-sound-transit, ticket-machines, the Mariners popular-destinations page, and bringing-your-bike. Complete dead ends with no onward paths.

Heuristic evaluation surfaced severity 4 issues — the highest — on service alerts. Alert icons were invisible on mobile. The system expansion map had a scroll trap (scrolling got captured by map zoom instead of the page). Users lost their trip planning state when navigating away. ORCA and transit jargon throughout the site assumed prior knowledge, especially a problem for tourists and first-time riders.

Card sorting (56 participants) revealed how riders actually group information — and it didn't map to the existing nav structure. "Ride with us" and "Get to know us" as categories had zero recognition.

Moderated usability testing (3 participants) showed "Plan a Trip" was consistently misread — users expected a journey planner tool, not general rider guidance. Emergency contact information was nearly unfindable, which is a safety issue, not just a UX one.

Tree testing (two rounds) let us validate the redesigned IA before presenting to stakeholders. Each round surfaced label mismatches we incorporated into revisions.

Competitive analysis across 8+ transit platforms benchmarked what functional transit IA looks like and identified patterns riders from other systems already know.

Who We Were Designing For

We developed five user personas across the project: first-time rider, SeaTac traveler, frequent commuter, event rider, and business/community user. These shaped which navigation failures were highest priority — a first-time rider hitting a dead-end page with no onward path is a much more critical failure than the same thing happening to a commuter who already knows the system.

What We Changed

We restructured the site around three clear audiences: Riders, Get Involved, and About Sound Transit.

Key structural decisions:

  • Alerts became a dedicated nav section — previously buried, now a top-level item given it's safety-critical information
  • Fares surfaced as its own nav item and linked directly from the Rider Guide, addressing the single most-searched content category
  • Maps moved to be accessible directly from the Rider section
  • "Plan a Trip" relabeled — testing showed the label set the wrong expectation
  • Rider Guide created — consolidated new-to-transit content that previously existed in scattered dead-end pages; includes getting an ORCA card, planning a trip, and rules and safety
  • Newsroom — Blog and News merged, Media Inquiries added
  • Doing Business With Us moved under About
  • Community Participation added under Get Involved, with Sounding Board and Event Calendar
  • Help moved to footer utility nav, consistent with how users expect support content to live
  • ST Shop renamed to Sound Transit Shop to reduce jargon
  • Emergency contacts surfaced — this one had real stakes

We also ran a co-design workshop with Sound Transit staff to pressure-test the taxonomy with the people who maintain the site — making sure our changes were implementable, not just conceptually clean.

First-time rider — Rider Guide

Fares was the most-searched content on the site — yet had no clear home in the navigation. We built a Rider Guide that walks someone through the whole journey in order: plan your trip, get an ORCA card, tap on and off. Scattered dead-end pages became one path for people who don't already know transit.

SeaTac traveler

"Airport" was among the top GA4 search terms — content that existed but wasn't reachable through navigation. We redesigned the SeaTac page for urgency: next train up top, popular destinations one tap away, and a "know before you travel" section for the things that actually trip people up.

Frequent commuter — Alerts

Heuristic evaluation flagged severity 4 issues on service alerts — icons invisible on mobile, information buried deep in the hierarchy. We pulled Alerts into its own dedicated navigation section so the first thing a commuter sees is exactly what they came for: is my train on time?

Event rider — Games and Events

The Mariners popular-destinations page had a 100% bounce rate — a complete dead end. We designed a Games and Events experience that puts tonight's game front and center: station options with walk times, train schedules, and extended service details in one tap.

Business and community

Not everyone on soundtransit.org is catching a train. We consolidated contracting under Doing Business With Us and gave Community Participation its own home under Get Involved — with the Sounding Board and Event Calendar surfaced together.

What I Did

I contributed across the full research arc. Specifically:

  • Led analysis on two rounds of tree testing data, tracking label failures across IA versions
  • Synthesized usability testing transcripts into a pin-up evaluation
  • Built the implementation roadmap, organizing research-backed changes into three tiers by effort and impact
  • Drafted rider-facing content for the Rider Guide (ORCA card steps, trip planning, safety and etiquette)
  • Presented findings to Sound Transit stakeholders and fielded Q&A at the UW HCDE capstone showcase

What It Taught Me

The stat that landed hardest with stakeholders wasn't a usability quote — it was 618,000 search sessions. Quantitative evidence opened the door; qualitative testing gave us the story to walk through it.

Co-designing with internal stakeholders also isn't optional for this kind of work. The taxonomy decisions that felt obvious to us as researchers weren't always implementable without buy-in from the people managing the CMS. Getting that alignment early meant our recommendations had a real path to shipping.
