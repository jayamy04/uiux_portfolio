Charter

Background

As Sound Transit grows, so do the information needs of the public, who range in audience type from passengers to business partners, to community members. ST has considered opportunities to improve the experience for users of soundtransit.org through a site refresh, including how to streamline the experience for the varied audiences that we serve. While discussions have taken place regarding the re-architecting of specific sections of the website, Sound Transit has not looked holistically at the discoverability of content under the existing navigation structure.

By partnering with a capstone team at UW HCDE, ST aims to gather data to inform a re-design effort and receive recommendations on how to modernize

Goals

  1. Goal 1

    Assess the discoverability of content under the existing IA

  2. Goal 2

    Understand how to better organize content to meet different audience needs, such as the UX of passengers, board members, partners, contractors and community members.

  3. Goal 3

    Inform the roadmap of a site-wide re-design

  4. Goal 4

    Develop user personas/ segments for soundtransit.org, and considerations for how to incorporate these personas into design practices

Scope

  1. Phase 1

    1) Phase 1: Project Plan: Students will work with the ST project team to develop a project plan for deliverables and schedule throughout the duration of the project. Students should develop an understanding of the current state practices of content production at Sound Transit and have a clear understanding of ST's goals for the capstone effort.

  2. Phase 2

    2) Phase 2: Research: Students should leverage best practices in UX research, data analysis, and information architecture to conduct a thorough audit of the existing site architecture. While the project deliverables will be specified in the project plan developed in phase 1, suggested research activities may include:

    • Content audit—inventory of all content/pages on the site, mapped to different audiences
    • Analysis of GA4 data
    • Best practices review with IA
    • Peer agency research
    • Persona/user segment development
    • Card sorting
    • Stakeholder interviews with the VEDD and Strategic Comms teams.
  3. Phase 3

    3) Phase 3: Design: Students should develop recommendations for how to improve site structure and navigation, based on the insights generated during the research phase. This may include the following:

    • Site architecture map
    • Revised taxonomy
    • Optional: few prototypes for new user experiences proposed
    • A roadmap of how to roll out incremental improvements
    • Iterations of deliverables, as tested and validated by stakeholder feedback and evaluative usability testing

Out of Scope

  • The work should be more conceptual design than visual design.
    • As such, an evaluation of the style guide and design system is out of scope. If necessary to convey recommended design changes that would follow a re-structuring, additional components can be proposed but should not be the focus of the re-design effort.
    • Similarly, the eventual verbiage used in any re-naming should be determined by the VEDD team. A proposed new taxonomy should use conceptual or descriptive labels, rather than take concern with the verbiage itself.

Assumptions

  • All deliverables received from the student team are considered as recommendations and do not commit Sound Transit to acting upon them.
  • The student team will have access to research tools to inform their work, including participant recruitment resources (UserLytics) and reports of Google Analytics data.
  • The project team should be available to provide guidance via a 30-60 minute meeting (weekly or bi-weekly). At the request of the student team, key stakeholder interviews or focus groups can be arranged with advanced notice.
  • The student team should be encouraged to think creatively, while understanding organizational needs and constraints. The partnership should be a collaborative partnership, rather than as a consulting agreement.

Website Redesign Usability Testing

Background

We are conducting user interviews to better understand how riders navigate the Sound Transit website, what information they prioritize, and where they experience confusion when navigating the website.

Our prior research methods have provided strong behavioral and structural insights:

  • GA4 analysis revealed common user paths and drop-off points
  • Heuristic evaluation identified usability issues
  • Content audit clarified key user groups and their Jobs-to-Be-Done (JTBD)
  • Competitive analysis highlighted how similar transit systems organize content
  • Card sorting revealed users' mental models for grouping information

However, these methods do not fully explain why users behave the way they do or how they interpret the current information architecture. User interviews will provide deeper insight into users' mental models, expectations, and pain points which will help us redesign the IA.

Research Goals

  • Understand how users conceptualize and navigate transit-related information
  • Identify mismatches between user mental models and the current IA
  • Discover key pain points, confusion points, and navigation breakdowns
  • Understand what information users prioritize and expect to find easily
  • Learn how users approach common tasks (e.g., trip planning, fares, service updates)
  • Validate and contextualize findings from GA4, card sorting, and heuristic evaluation

Research Questions

  1. 01

    What are the most common tasks users come to the website to complete, and what information are they looking for?

  2. 02

    Which tasks are easiest for users to complete, and which are the most difficult? How do users currently work around any challenges?

  3. 03

    Where do users experience the most confusion or friction, and where do they experience the least?

  4. 04

    What terminology or labels are most clear to users, and which ones cause the most confusion?

  5. 05

    What aspects of the current site structure are working well, if any?

Target User Groups

  • Frequent Sound Transit Riders
  • At least 18 years old

Research Method and Setup

Semi-structured user interviews (30–45 minutes)

Setup

Sample size: 5 participants

Recruitment:

  • UW HCDE students from capstone class

Testing Protocol

Opening

Hi [name], thanks so much for joining me today. My name is [your name] and I'm a UX researcher working on a project with Sound Transit. Today we're looking at their website — specifically, how easy it is for people to find what they need.

I want to be clear: we are testing the website, not you. There are no right or wrong answers. If you get confused or stuck, that is actually useful information for us.

Please think out loud as you go — just narrate what you're looking at, what you're thinking, and what you expect to happen. It helps us understand your experience.

We'll be recording the session today for research purposes only. The recording won't be shared outside the research team. Do you have any questions before we get started?

Pre-Interview Questions

  1. How often do you use the Sound Transit website, and what do you typically use it for?

  2. Can you walk me through a recent time you used it? What were you trying to do?

  3. What other tools or technologies do you use to get around, if any?

Scenarios

Your Own Experience as a Rider

These next scenarios are about your own experience using the website.

  1. You need to get to SeaTac Airport before 1pm tomorrow. How would you plan that trip?

    What made you start there?

    Was there anything that wasn't where you expected it to be?

  2. You want to check the schedule for the 1 Line running between Lynnwood and Federal Way. How would you find that?

    Did the labels or names used on the site match what you expected?

    Was there anything confusing about how the schedules were organized?

  3. It's winter and you've been hearing about snow and storms next week, and you want to know if it will affect your travel. How would you find out, and how would you make sure you're notified if something changes?

    Was anything unclear about how to stay up to date on service disruptions?

  4. You're waiting at a bus stop for Route 542 toward Bellevue and the bus hasn't shown up when expected. Using the website, how would you check whether there are any delays or alerts affecting that route?

    Was the information you found what you were looking for?

    How does this compare to what you normally do when a bus is late?

  5. You heard Sound Transit is planning to extend light rail to Everett and West Seattle. Where would you go to learn more about the status of those projects and what the timeline looks like?

    Did the website make it clear where to find this information? What would have made it easier?

Helping Someone New to Sound Transit

Now imagine a friend is visiting Seattle for the first time and wants to use Sound Transit to get around. You're helping them navigate the website.

  1. Your friend is arriving at Sea-Tac Airport and wants to take transit to get to downtown Seattle. Where would you show them on the website to help them figure out how?

    Was the information easy to find for someone who has never taken the transit?

    Is there anything you wish the site had made clearer?

  2. Your friend wants to understand how to pay — things like whether they need a card, how much it costs, and how to add money. Where would you show them on the website?

    Was anything confusing about the payment information?

  3. Your friend wants to explore the city and is wondering where transit can actually take them. How would you help them discover popular destinations using the website?

    Did you find what you were looking for, or did you expect something different?

  4. Your friend wants to know what to expect when riding — things like what's allowed on board and what amenities are available at stations. Where would you look?

    Did the section names on the site match what you expected?

    Was there anything you think your friend would still be left wondering after visiting this page?

Beyond Riding

A couple more scenarios — these are about other things people sometimes come to the Sound Transit website for.

  1. Please go to the System Performance Tracker page. Take a moment to look around — what do you notice? What do you think this page is for?

    Then, find the "Ridership" link and click on it. What do you see?

    Is this what you expected to find here?

  2. Imagine you run a small construction consulting business. You are curious whether there are opportunities to work with Sound Transit — for example, as a vendor or contractor. Where would you go to find that information?

    Did the website make it clear where to find this information? What would have made it easier?

Reflection

  1. Were there any moments where you were not sure what to do next?

  2. What worked well for you, if anything?

  3. Were there any moments when you felt stuck? What would have helped you move forward?

  4. Based on what you saw today, what is the one thing you would most want to improve about the website?

  5. Is there anything else you would like to share that we have not asked about?

Findings

We tested a mid-fi prototype of the redesigned IA across rider and community/business sessions.

All three participants found the top-level separation between Riders, Projects, and About directionally correct — the structural decisions held. The failures were label-level and interaction-level.

Emergency contacts

All three participants failed to find emergency contact information. The "?" button was interpreted as a chatbot by all three. P3 said she "would not go there if it was urgent." This is a safety-critical failure, not just a UX one.

Contact Us

All three participants looked for contact in the footer or utility nav first. P3 said she would look for the footer of the site before anywhere else → Customer Service & Contact surfaced under Help in the final IA.

Fares

P2 said getting an ORCA card is a different task from seeing fare prices.

"Get Involved" label

P2 said it was muddy and unclear, and suggested renaming to Work With Us. P3 independently said the same thing — that it wasn't clear both business and rider users could "Get Involved," and suggested Work With Us as a rename. Two participants landing on the same rename unprompted was a strong signal.

Blog and News placement

All three participants independently said Blog and News belong under About, not Get Involved. P2 said News in Get Involved felt like it was for collaborators, not the public. P3 said if the content is company updates, it should be in About.

Community Participation label

P1 said Community Events sounded like rider entertainment — similar to Games and Events for Riders — and couldn't distinguish civic participation from event entertainment based on the label alone. This matched tree testing, where 16 out of 24 participants could not find it.

Jobs

P3 said Jobs feels more like it belongs under About.

Findings

Community Engagement

Community engagement staff described audiences spanning youth riders and teachers, property owners affected by construction, vendors seeking contracting opportunities, and community members trying to follow expansion projects. Seth and Daphne focus on safety engagement — crossing safety, school presentations, and evergreen safety content that makes up roughly 70% of their messaging — while Sagar and Harrison manage capital project outreach across corridors including Crosslake, the Central Corridor, and communities south of SeaTac Airport.

Navigation emerged as a consistent pain point. Harrison described the site as "not the most intuitive" and noted that project pages require excessive clicking and scrolling to reach documents — for example, the West Seattle Link Extension documents page. Property owners who received impact letters years ago often need deep links to specific environmental or planning materials rather than broad landing pages. Sagar echoed that documents become messy over time, there is no search function, and contact cards and page structures lack standardization. Both suggested additional filters for environmental documents, technical reports, public engagement reports, and maps showing how route designs evolved over time.

Project pages serve as the primary hub for community engagement, combining digital fact sheets, milestone updates, board decisions, and links to online open houses hosted on third-party platforms. GovDelivery newsletters push subscribers to project updates and construction alerts, but past materials — such as Federal Way project content or archived online open houses — remain available only if users know the direct URL and are not surfaced on system expansion landing pages. Harrison described community input as a funnel that narrows from broad alternatives discussion through planning into construction, and noted that the site sometimes presents phase transitions awkwardly — for example, the Connecting to Stations page under Creating Vibrant Stations.

Content maintenance varies widely by page type. Evergreen program pages may go untouched for five or more years, while milestone-driven project pages get updated when board decisions or outreach events occur through a collaborative workflow with the web team. Seth and Daphne update safety program content roughly once a year, though politically sensitive pages like at-grade crossing hazard mitigation can take a month to update depending on the current political climate. Katie noted that board members, as elected officials, influence which issues receive visibility — content relevance can shift with campaign cycles — and that on-the-go information needs should appear in both rider and community contexts, potentially on the homepage.

Repeated inbound requests center on contracting opportunities, technical documents within large environmental impact statements (EIS), and property development questions. Sagar noted that finding a specific detail — such as noise impacts to a particular property — within a gigantic EIS is difficult enough that staff often send page numbers and explainer language directly. Harrison prioritized highlighting the latest milestone planning document on project pages, since capital project audiences most need current planning materials rather than outdated construction-era content. Seth emphasized that safety messaging aimed at changing behavior requires more than simplified information.

Technical SME

Ian (digital specialist) and Jera (software engineer) maintain the site day to day, handling content updates, navigation changes, bug fixes, and new page creation. Alerts are updated most frequently, along with manually created project updates, construction alerts, news, blog posts, and board or subcommittee meeting pages. Ian serves as the point of contact for communication requests, while larger program updates flow through Word document collaboration, content sign-off, and staging before going live.

The current site structure grew organically: every new page requires choosing a parent page, which adds the page to the navigation tree stored in a main-menu → sub-page → table-of-contents hierarchy. Ian noted that deciding where a new page belongs is straightforward, but the technical implementation of building pages is difficult. The homepage serves riders seeking trip planning and service alerts, with secondary information layered beneath.

The most significant IA constraint is a two-level-deep navigation limit — content beyond two levels is presented through table-of-contents-style layouts with no dedicated subpage navigation. Jobs, for example, has no direct nav entry. Ian emphasized that every page should maintain indirect links back to the main menu. Maps appear in three locations including under "Get to Know Us," despite receiving heavy rider traffic — the brand section duplicates the same maps at Our Brand → Maps. Ian acknowledged this placement is problematic and that light rail schedules are an area the team wants to improve.

Low-traffic pages must still be maintained for regulatory compliance (civil rights, Title IX, and similar requirements). A navigation redesign would need to preserve breadcrumb links, URL paths, and SEO value so nothing breaks. Ian confirmed that technically, anything is possible — the constraints are organizational rather than platform-level.

When asked what they would change, Ian prioritized a more streamlined information architecture and bringing third-party online open house content in-house — platforms like Ballard Link's participate.online cannot support the depth of engagement materials the team wants to host directly on soundtransit.org.

Passenger Experience

The Passenger Experience team handles rider inquiries across phone, feedback, and other channels, and relies on the website as a key reference when directing riders to self-service information. The team uses a third-party alerting system (IBI Arcadus) with constraints on how alert information can be displayed on the site.

The clearest finding from the session was schedule discoverability: when asked what they would change about the site, the team pointed to finding schedules as requiring too many clicks. This aligns with the broader pattern across stakeholder groups that rider-facing task flows — particularly schedules, maps, and fare information — are buried deeper than users expect.
