Sound Transit Website Redesign

Sound Transit Digital Web Team

Background

As Sound Transit grows, so do the information needs of the public, who range in audience type from passengers to business partners, to community members. ST has considered opportunities to improve the experience for users of soundtransit.org through a site refresh, including how to streamline the experience for the varied audiences that we serve. While discussions have taken place regarding the re-architecting of specific sections of the website, Sound Transit has not looked holistically at the discoverability of content under the existing navigation structure.

By partnering with a capstone team at UW HCDE, ST aims to gather data to inform a re-design effort and receive recommendations on how to modernize

Goals

  1. Goal 1

    Assess the discoverability of content under the existing IA

  2. Goal 2

    Understand how to better organize content to meet different audience needs, such as the UX of passengers, board members, partners, contractors and community members.

  3. Goal 3

    Inform the roadmap of a site-wide re-design

  4. Goal 4

    Develop user personas/ segments for soundtransit.org, and considerations for how to incorporate these personas into design practices

Stakeholders

  • VEDD (Video, Editorial, Digital and Design)

Website Redesign Usability Testing

Background

We are conducting user interviews to better understand how riders navigate the Sound Transit website, what information they prioritize, and where they experience confusion when navigating the website.

Our prior research methods have provided strong behavioral and structural insights:

  • GA4 analysis revealed common user paths and drop-off points
  • Heuristic evaluation identified usability issues
  • Content audit clarified key user groups and their Jobs-to-Be-Done (JTBD)
  • Competitive analysis highlighted how similar transit systems organize content
  • Card sorting revealed users' mental models for grouping information

However, these methods do not fully explain why users behave the way they do or how they interpret the current information architecture. User interviews will provide deeper insight into users' mental models, expectations, and pain points which will help us redesign the IA.

Research Goals

  • Understand how users conceptualize and navigate transit-related information
  • Identify mismatches between user mental models and the current IA
  • Discover key pain points, confusion points, and navigation breakdowns
  • Understand what information users prioritize and expect to find easily
  • Learn how users approach common tasks (e.g., trip planning, fares, service updates)
  • Validate and contextualize findings from GA4, card sorting, and heuristic evaluation

Research Questions

  1. 01

    What are the most common tasks users come to the website to complete, and what information are they looking for?

  2. 02

    Which tasks are easiest for users to complete, and which are the most difficult? How do users currently work around any challenges?

  3. 03

    Where do users experience the most confusion or friction, and where do they experience the least?

  4. 04

    What terminology or labels are most clear to users, and which ones cause the most confusion?

  5. 05

    What aspects of the current site structure are working well, if any?

Target User Groups

  • Frequent Sound Transit Riders
  • At least 18 years old

Research Method and Setup

Semi-structured user interviews (30–45 minutes)

Setup

Sample size: 6 participants

Recruitment:

  • UW HCDE students from capstone class

Testing Protocol

Opening

Hi [name], thanks so much for joining me today. My name is [your name] and I'm a UX researcher working on a project with Sound Transit. Today we're looking at their website — specifically, how easy it is for people to find what they need.

I want to be clear: we are testing the website, not you. There are no right or wrong answers. If you get confused or stuck, that is actually useful information for us.

Please think out loud as you go — just narrate what you're looking at, what you're thinking, and what you expect to happen. It helps us understand your experience.

We'll be recording the session today for research purposes only. The recording won't be shared outside the research team. Do you have any questions before we get started?

  1. How often do you use the Sound Transit website, and what do you typically use it for?

  2. Can you walk me through a recent time you used it? What were you trying to do?

  3. What other tools or technologies do you use to get around, if any?

Scenarios

Your Own Experience as a Rider

These next scenarios are about your own experience using the website.

  1. You need to get to SeaTac Airport before 1pm tomorrow. How would you plan that trip?

    What made you start there?

    Was there anything that wasn't where you expected it to be?

  2. You want to check the schedule for the 1 Line running between Lynnwood and Federal Way. How would you find that?

    Was there anything confusing about how the schedules were organized?

  3. It's winter and you've been hearing about snow and storms next week, and you want to know if it will affect your travel. How would you find out, and how would you make sure you're notified if something changes?

  4. You're waiting at a bus stop for Route 542 toward Bellevue and the bus hasn't shown up when expected. Using the website, how would you check whether there are any delays or alerts affecting that route?

    Was the information you found what you were looking for?

    How does this compare to what you normally do when a bus is late?

  5. You heard Sound Transit is planning to extend light rail to Everett and West Seattle. Where would you go to learn more about the status of those projects and what the timeline looks like?

    Did the website make it clear where to find this information? What would have made it easier?

    Was the information easy to find for someone who has never taken the transit?

    Is there anything you wish the site had made clearer?

  2. Your friend wants to understand how to pay — things like whether they need a card, how much it costs, and how to add money. Where would you show them on the website?

    Was anything confusing about the payment information?

  3. Your friend wants to explore the city and is wondering where transit can actually take them. How would you help them discover popular destinations using the website?

    Did you find what you were looking for, or did you expect something different?

  4. Your friend wants to know what to expect when riding — things like what's allowed on board and what amenities are available at stations. Where would you look?

    Did the section names on the site match what you expected?

    Was there anything you think your friend would still be left wondering after visiting this page?

Findings

Navigation & Information Architecture

Across six moderated sessions (one desktop, five mobile), participants consistently described primary navigation as hidden behind too many clicks and nested submenus. When browsing failed, nearly all turned to the site search bar as a fallback — often successfully, but only after abandoning the menu structure.

Section naming mismatched mental models: rider-facing tasks buried under labels like "Get to know us" sent participants down the wrong paths. On mobile, the long side navigation was occasionally helpful for scanning, but its depth reinforced the sense that key tasks were not surfaced at the top level.

Plan Your Trip

Trip planning showed mixed success. Some participants completed airport and downtown routing easily; others hit errors, unclear loading states, or gave up on mobile. Dense instructional text and announcement-style modules that resembled buttons added friction before participants reached actionable results.

Location search failed for common place-name variants — Westlake, SeaTac, downtown Seattle — because the tool expects station names rather than destinations or landmarks. Participants wanted Google Maps– or Apple Maps–style landmark search. When real-time updates, cost estimates, or walking directions appeared, participants valued them — but button loading and error states were often ambiguous.

Saving a route as PDF was not useful; participants wanted to save a trip for when they were actually leaving.

Find Schedule & the 1 Line

The homepage schedule widget surfaced alerts rather than schedules, setting the wrong expectation before participants even reached timetable content. Schedule tables felt overwhelming — dense columns of numbers, horizontal scroll on mobile, and PDF downloads that read as outdated.

Direction labels confused non-local riders: northbound/southbound did not map cleanly to toward Lynnwood or Federal Way. Line icons offered little help without local knowledge. Some participants preferred a point-to-point schedule view over scanning full-line tables.

Alerts & Service Disruptions

Homepage alerts were not consistently clickable and were easy to confuse with general announcements. Alert detail pages read like articles rather than actionable riding guidance — too many clicks to reach route-specific impact, with subscribe and plus buttons buried below long copy.

Unrelated positive alerts (e.g., Crosslake project news) appeared alongside service disruptions and added noise. Participants wanted route-filtered alerts and text subscriptions, but raised concerns about relevance if notifications were not scoped to lines they actually ride.

Popular Destinations & New Riders

Popular destinations were hard to find without search. Content skewed toward articles and stadiums rather than tourist landmarks participants expected — Pike Place Market, the Space Needle. Planning a trip from a destination page lacked context and pre-filled routing.

One participant had success with a lifestyle-oriented page that framed destinations around how to get there. New riders helping a visiting friend were often overwhelmed; experienced riders wanted faster paths and personalization instead of re-reading introductory content.

How to Pay & Know Before You Go

Payment information was generally findable across three tabs, though embedded videos ran long for quick lookup. Rules and riding etiquette required too many clicks; accordion dropdowns hid content participants expected to scan on one page. Station amenities were particularly hard to locate — several participants never found them without search or researcher prompting.

System Expansion & Projects

System expansion was among the easiest areas to find. The expansion map received consistent praise for making corridor work legible at a glance.

Participants debated list vs. map views; map interactions on mobile had friction (zoom behavior, two-finger popups blocking content). Individual project pages led with cost and status while opening dates and timelines — what participants wanted first — appeared only after scrolling through dense prose.

Performance Data, Ridership & Business Content

The System Performance Tracker felt hidden; participants relied on search to reach it. Data visualizations served analyst-style use cases more than typical riders, and the page title set expectations for principles or accountability metrics rather than operational performance data.

Vendor and contracting information was eventually found under "Get to know us" → "Doing business with us," but labels conflated recruitment with vendor opportunities. Several participants confused hiring content with procurement paths.

Comparison to Other Tools & Overall Experience

Participants routinely compared soundtransit.org unfavorably to Google Maps, Apple Maps, OneBusAway, and Transit for real-time routing. The website felt static next to map apps that answer "when do I leave?" in one step.

The sitewide feedback button drew attention but its scope was unclear — participants were unsure whether it was for trip issues, website bugs, or general comments. Mobile schedule columns were difficult to read at default sizes, compounding table-heavy tasks.

Findings

Outreach & Expansion Projects

Participants described audiences spanning youth riders and teachers, property owners affected by construction, vendors seeking contracting opportunities, and community members trying to follow expansion projects. Some focus on safety engagement — crossing safety, school presentations, and evergreen safety content that makes up roughly 70% of their messaging — while others manage capital project outreach across corridors including Crosslake, the Central Corridor, and communities south of SeaTac Airport.

Navigation emerged as a consistent pain point. One participant described the site as "not the most intuitive" and noted that project pages require excessive clicking and scrolling to reach documents — for example, the West Seattle Link Extension documents page. Property owners who received impact letters years ago often need deep links to specific environmental or planning materials rather than broad landing pages. Others echoed that documents become messy over time, there is no search function, and contact cards and page structures lack standardization. Participants suggested additional filters for environmental documents, technical reports, public engagement reports, and maps showing how route designs evolved over time.

Project pages serve as the primary hub for community engagement, combining digital fact sheets, milestone updates, board decisions, and links to online open houses hosted on third-party platforms. GovDelivery newsletters push subscribers to project updates and construction alerts, but past materials — such as Federal Way project content or archived online open houses — remain available only if users know the direct URL and are not surfaced on system expansion landing pages. One participant described community input as a funnel that narrows from broad alternatives discussion through planning into construction, and noted that the site sometimes presents phase transitions awkwardly — for example, the Connecting to Stations page under Creating Vibrant Stations.

Content maintenance varies widely by page type. Evergreen program pages may go untouched for five or more years, while milestone-driven project pages get updated when board decisions or outreach events occur through a collaborative workflow with the web team. Safety program content is updated roughly once a year, though politically sensitive pages like at-grade crossing hazard mitigation can take a month to update depending on the current political climate. One participant noted that board members, as elected officials, influence which issues receive visibility — content relevance can shift with campaign cycles — and that on-the-go information needs should appear in both rider and community contexts, potentially on the homepage.

Repeated inbound requests center on contracting opportunities, technical documents within large environmental impact statements (EIS), and property development questions. Finding a specific detail — such as noise impacts to a particular property — within a gigantic EIS is difficult enough that staff often send page numbers and explainer language directly. Participants prioritized highlighting the latest milestone planning document on project pages, since capital project audiences most need current planning materials rather than outdated construction-era content. Safety messaging aimed at changing behavior requires more than simplified information.

Site Maintenance & Information Architecture

Participants who maintain the site day to day handle content updates, navigation changes, bug fixes, and new page creation. Alerts are updated most frequently, along with manually created project updates, construction alerts, news, blog posts, and board or subcommittee meeting pages. One participant serves as the point of contact for communication requests, while larger program updates flow through Word document collaboration, content sign-off, and staging before going live.

The current site structure grew organically: every new page requires choosing a parent page, which adds the page to the navigation tree stored in a main-menu → sub-page → table-of-contents hierarchy. Deciding where a new page belongs is straightforward, but the technical implementation of building pages is difficult. The homepage serves riders seeking trip planning and service alerts, with secondary information layered beneath.

The most significant IA constraint is a two-level-deep navigation limit — content beyond two levels is presented through table-of-contents-style layouts with no dedicated subpage navigation. Jobs, for example, has no direct nav entry. Every page should maintain indirect links back to the main menu. Maps appear in three locations including under "Get to Know Us," despite receiving heavy rider traffic — the brand section duplicates the same maps at Our Brand → Maps. This placement is problematic, and light rail schedules are an area participants want to improve.

Low-traffic pages must still be maintained for regulatory compliance (civil rights, Title IX, and similar requirements). A navigation redesign would need to preserve breadcrumb links, URL paths, and SEO value so nothing breaks. Technically, anything is possible — the constraints are organizational rather than platform-level.

When asked what they would change, participants prioritized a more streamlined information architecture and bringing third-party online open house content in-house — platforms like Ballard Link's participate.online cannot support the depth of engagement materials they want to host directly on soundtransit.org.

Rider Support & Service

Participants who handle rider inquiries across phone, feedback, and other channels rely on the website as a key reference when directing riders to self-service information. They use a third-party alerting system (IBI Arcadus) with constraints on how alert information can be displayed on the site.

The clearest finding was schedule discoverability: when asked what they would change about the site, participants pointed to finding schedules as requiring too many clicks. This aligns with the broader pattern across stakeholder groups that rider-facing task flows — particularly schedules, maps, and fare information — are buried deeper than users expect.
