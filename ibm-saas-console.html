IBM Client Engineering — San Francisco · Summer 2023

Facilitating enterprise discovery: getting to the real problem before anyone touches a solution

Role
Innovation Designer (UX Research + Facilitation lead)
Team
Mark Ainscow (Innovation Designer), Brian Calder (Lead UX), Rami Alayan (Design Manager)
Timeline
Summer 2023 · 12 weeks
Methods
Facilitated workshops · Empathy mapping · Architecture assessment · Stakeholder alignment · Content strategy

Context

IBM's Client Engineering team works at the earliest stage of any client engagement — before a solution is chosen, before development begins, sometimes before the client even knows what their real problem is. My role was to design and run the research sessions that gave clients a clear picture of where they actually stood. I worked across two healthcare organizations and one internal product redesign over the course of the summer.

The core challenge in enterprise discovery is the same every time: clients arrive with assumptions baked in. They've already decided what they need. The researcher's job is to slow that down — to create enough structured space that the real constraints and gaps can surface before anyone commits to a direction.

CVS Health — cloud migration readiness

CVS Health, one of the largest healthcare companies in the US with 300K+ employees, wanted to move from on-premise infrastructure to the cloud. The conversation kept gravitating toward migration timelines and tooling choices. My job was to redirect it: before anyone talks about how to migrate, we need to understand what we're actually migrating.

Research approach

I designed a structured readiness assessment as a facilitated Mural workshop. The session was built around four areas: existing architecture and software dependencies, custom application compatibility, data services and pipelines, and disaster recovery setup. The goal wasn't to audit their systems — it was to make the current state visible and shared across stakeholders who often had different mental models of what they had.

This matters because in large enterprises, technical stakeholders and business stakeholders frequently operate on different assumptions about what's ready. Making those assumptions explicit in a shared workspace — rather than letting them surface later as blockers — is where facilitated research creates real value.

What structured discovery does that a requirements doc doesn't

A requirements document captures what people think they need. A facilitated workshop surfaces what they don't know they don't know. The architecture mapping process revealed dependencies and compatibility gaps that hadn't been formally documented, and exposed places where different stakeholders had meaningfully different understandings of the same system. Those gaps needed to be resolved before any migration sequencing could be trusted.

From research to roadmap

The assessment findings shaped a co-created migration plan: sequencing work in order of readiness, starting with FileNet Content Manager before moving to container deployment, operator installation, and post-deployment tasks. The research gave CVS a concrete, de-risked path forward instead of an overwhelming leap — and gave IBM a defensible basis for the recommendations.

MCNA / UnitedHealth Group — discovery before solutions

MCNA came in with a different problem: they were confident in their solutions but hadn't done the discovery work to justify that confidence. They knew what they wanted to buy. They didn't know whether it would actually address what was breaking.

Research approach

I designed a discovery workshop around a deliberately open question: what are the issues you're actually facing? The session structure was built to prevent anchoring — if you open a stakeholder session by showing them a potential solution, everything they say after that is shaped by it. Starting from problems rather than solutions creates space for things to surface that the client hadn't thought to name.

The workshop used empathy mapping as its backbone: who are the actual users of these workflows, what are they trying to accomplish, what does success look like from their perspective, and what's getting in the way. This framework is useful precisely because it forces stakeholders to speak from users' perspectives rather than system perspectives — and that shift in framing consistently reveals gaps that aren't visible when you're describing how a process works rather than how it's experienced.

What emerged

The session surfaced that several significant workflow bottlenecks weren't people problems — they were process automation gaps. Tasks being done manually had no technical reason to stay manual; they'd simply never been scoped for automation. The real failure mode was invisible without cross-functional discovery because each team experienced their piece of it but no one had mapped the handoffs.

Research-backed recommendation

With a grounded evidence base, the team could make a confident recommendation: IBM Watson Orchestrate, a workflow orchestration tool that addressed the specific handoff and automation gaps the research had surfaced. The difference between recommending a tool because it fits a pattern and recommending it because it addresses findings from a structured discovery session is the difference between a sales pitch and a research-backed proposal.

IBM SaaS Console — notification research

Later in the engagement I joined Lead UX Designer Brian Calder and Design Manager Rami Alayan on an internal product problem: enterprise clients were missing critical alerts when trials expired or subscriptions ran into issues. The notifications existed. They weren't working.

The research question

Before any redesign could happen, the team needed to understand why the alerts weren't landing. The problem could have been visibility — alerts buried in the wrong place. It could have been clarity — users seeing notifications but not understanding what they meant. Or it could have been language — alerts that were too vague to act on. The answer shaped whether this was primarily an IA problem, a visual design problem, or a content problem.

Research process

I audited the existing notification system, mapping where alerts appeared, what language they used, and what action — if any — they prompted. I also looked at how enterprise users typically encounter and process system status information, and where the existing alerts broke down against that mental model.

The diagnosis pointed most strongly at language and information hierarchy. The existing notifications described system states but didn't tell users what those states meant for them or what to do next. An alert that says "your trial has ended" is technically accurate but tells the user nothing about what to do next. Subscription and trial status was also buried within a general notifications feed rather than surfaced where users would encounter it naturally.

Redesign and content strategy

I redesigned the notification dashboard to surface subscription and trial status clearly, and wrote all notification content from scratch. The writing principle was simple: every notification should answer three questions — what is the status, what does it mean for you, and what should you do right now. Vague system-state descriptions became specific, actionable alerts with language calibrated to the urgency of the situation.

Content strategy in enterprise software is an underrated research skill. The gap between an alert that users act on and one they ignore is almost always a language problem — specificity, framing, and the right level of urgency. Getting that right requires understanding users' mental models well enough to know what they need to hear, not just what's technically true.

The redesign was approved by stakeholders and handed off for development before the engagement concluded.

What enterprise research taught me

Enterprise discovery is slower and messier than consumer research. Clients have politics, legacy systems, and years of accumulated assumptions. The researcher's job isn't just to surface insights — it's to create the conditions where insights can surface at all. That means designing sessions that slow people down, ask them to articulate things they've never had to say out loud, and make the current state visible before anyone starts imagining the future state.

The other thing enterprise research taught me: recommendations without evidence are just opinions. The difference between IBM telling a client what to do and IBM helping a client understand what they need is entirely a function of how rigorously the discovery was done.

Outside the project, I spent my summer getting to know IBM itself — community events, coffee chats across teams — which gave me a feel for how insights actually travel through a big organization.
