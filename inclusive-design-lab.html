The question

Public art is everywhere — murals, mosaics, sculptures embedded in the places we move through every day. But almost all of it assumes you can see it.

Museums have audio guides, tactile tours, and dedicated accessibility staff. Urban public art has none of that. It's decentralized, uncatalogued, and often discovered by accident — a mode of discovery that's entirely inaccessible to blind and low-vision (BLV) people.

Prior research had studied art accessibility in controlled settings like museums, and urban accessibility research had focused on navigation. Nobody had studied the intersection: how BLV people discover and engage with art in dynamic urban spaces.

We set out to fill that gap.

Methods

Building a design space for urban public art

Before we could study how BLV people experience urban public art, we needed a shared vocabulary for talking about it. Urban art is wildly varied — a tiny mosaic on a transit staircase is a completely different experience than a 30-foot sculpture in a public plaza. Without a framework to characterize those differences, our interview discussions would have been too abstract to be useful.

So we built one. Drawing on public art theory and urban accessibility literature, we defined seven dimensions that capture what makes any given public art encounter distinct:

Art attributes — properties of the artwork itself:

  • Type — mural, mosaic, or sculpture. Each has different tactile and descriptive properties. A mural can't be touched; a sculpture might be.
  • Scale — how big it is, from small enough to hold in your hands to larger than a building (Cloud Gate in Chicago is 33 feet high and 66 feet long).
  • Tactility — can you actually physically reach and touch it? A ground-level sculpture is different from a mural painted 20 feet up a wall, or a fountain where the sculptural elements are surrounded by water.
  • Artist purpose — is it a monument, a community statement, an amenity, or purely decorative? Purpose shapes what an accessible description should even prioritize.

Engagement experience — contextual factors that change with every visit:

  • Visitor goal — are you there specifically to see art, or did you encounter it on a commute? A purposeful cultural visit versus a serendipitous discovery call for completely different kinds of support.
  • Crowd density — a quiet corner of a park versus a busy transit station during rush hour creates completely different constraints for a BLV person trying to stop and engage with art.
  • Sociability — are you alone (and relying entirely on technology) or with others (who might describe it for you, but whose help comes with its own social dynamics)?

The reason this mattered: we used these seven dimensions to deliberately design six artwork probes that varied across the space — different types, sizes, locations, and crowd contexts. That meant our findings weren't limited to one kind of art in one kind of setting. We could compare how participant needs and priorities shifted depending on context, which made the resulting design implications much more actionable than a single-setting study would have produced.

Semi-structured interviews with design probes

We conducted 16 semi-structured interviews with BLV participants recruited across the US through disability organizations and mailing lists. Participants ranged from 26 to 91 years old, spanned a wide range of vision experiences — born completely blind, lost vision gradually, partial peripheral vision — and included two participants who identified as artists themselves. We didn't require participants to have prior interest in public art; we wanted perspectives from people across the full spectrum of engagement. Sessions were 90 minutes each, conducted remotely over Zoom to allow geographic diversity and make it practical to present diverse artwork probes.

Each interview had two parts:

Part 1 — Prior experiences. We asked participants how they currently encounter and engage with public art: how they find out about it, what barriers they face, how access differs between urban and museum settings, and what factors reduce their interest or engagement. We also asked how their goals in urban spaces differed from visits to galleries — because a commuter passing a mural has fundamentally different needs than someone who traveled to a museum specifically to engage with art.

Part 2 — Design exploration with probes. We walked participants through six real urban artworks — murals, mosaics, and sculptures selected to represent diverse points across our design space. For each probe, we first read an AI-generated short description aloud, then invited reactions, ideation about multisensory access solutions, and live follow-up questions to an AI system (Claude 3.7 Sonnet), whose responses we read back verbatim. We intentionally left two AI descriptions with factual errors uncorrected to see how participants responded to inaccuracies in real time — which turned out to be one of the most generative parts of the study.

After all six probes, we reflected on preferred methods for learning about, navigating to, and engaging with public art, and closed with questions about AI's role and how context shaped what information participants needed.

Thematic analysis

We recorded and transcribed all 16 sessions. Using codebook thematic analysis, the first author developed a preliminary codebook through hybrid coding — inductive codes capturing participant-driven ideas (like spontaneous art discovery or safety concerns) alongside deductive codes tied to our design space dimensions. The codebook was reviewed and refined with all co-authors, and included a distinction between participant-generated suggestions and reactions to technologies we introduced — important for separating what participants organically wanted from what they responded to when prompted.

I reviewed a randomly selected half of the coded transcripts, flagging missed codes or unclear applications, which we resolved through discussion. We also coded all AI outputs for factual accuracy separately — assessing each description attribute as accurate, omitted, or inaccurate, and tracking how the live AI responses handled participant questions (including cases where the system produced inaccuracies or declined to answer).

What we found

Urban art is valued differently than museum art — and the access gap is different too

Participants didn't just want the same museum accessibility tools transplanted outdoors. They valued urban art specifically because it was free, approachable, community-rooted, and serendipitous. One participant described encountering a fountain by accident: it was "kind of fun just to be there and listening to the water and the kids playing." That organic, spontaneous experience was exactly what was missing for BLV participants — and what any future technology needed to replicate, not replace.

Spontaneous discovery is the core access gap

Almost every participant (15 of 16) wanted location-aware notifications to alert them to nearby artwork. Sighted people turn a corner and discover a mural. BLV participants had no equivalent. They wanted proximity-based alerts — some imagined being notified within 10 feet of a piece — but were clear that safety came first: any audio from art descriptions couldn't interfere with navigation cues, traffic signals, or ambient awareness.

Safety shapes everything

Twelve participants flagged safety as a primary constraint. Urban art exists in unpredictable environments — busy intersections, transit stairways, crowded markets. A mosaic on a transit station staircase during rush hour isn't a place anyone can stop to engage with art.

Multisensory access has real potential — and real constraints

All 16 participants wanted to use touch to engage with artwork independently. They appreciated that public art, unlike museum art, isn't "behind glass." But urban art created its own tactile barriers: murals can't be touched, sculptures are often too large or elevated to grasp fully, and some locations are unsafe to linger in. Participants suggested 3D scaled replicas, tactile maps, and haptic feedback for large murals — while also flagging practical concerns like weather damage, vandalism, and the cost of retrofitting existing infrastructure.

Fourteen participants supported spatial audio for navigation to artwork. Ten wanted music integrated into culturally significant pieces. Some suggested scent as a discovery mechanism. But across all of these: concern about disrupting others in shared public space.

AI descriptions are promising — but cultural accuracy is non-negotiable

All 16 participants were at least somewhat positive about AI-generated descriptions. The ability to ask follow-up questions in real time was particularly valued — it let participants direct the interaction toward what they cared about, rather than receiving a generic overview.

But accuracy errors hit differently with culturally significant art. One probe — an Indigenous American mosaic at a transit station — had its canoe misdescribed as a "rocket shape." When participants learned about the error, the response was visceral:

Design implications

The research pointed to four directions for future technology:

Context-aware discovery. Notifications need to be adaptive to safety context — different behavior at a street crossing versus a quiet park, with clear user control over when and how alerts surface.

Culturally grounded descriptions. AI descriptions of public art need to prioritize cultural and historical context, not just visual inventory. The site-specific nature of urban art means a generic description often misses the point entirely.

Multisensory infrastructure. 3D replicas, tactile maps, and spatial audio have real potential — but need to be designed for urban realities: weather, vandalism, public space etiquette, and the hands-full reality of cane or guide dog users.

Expanding urban access beyond navigation. Most BLV urban accessibility research is about getting from A to B safely. This work argues that cultural and aesthetic participation in the built environment is also a right — and a design problem worth solving.
