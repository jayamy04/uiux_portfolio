Motivation

Most prior work on accessible visual art has primarily focused on fine art in a museum or gallery setting. However, there are distinct differences between art in controlled museum environments, where art is carefully curated and surroundings are generally quiet, and urban settings, where art can be situated in a wide range of settings and can take a vast array of different forms.

There is limited understanding on how to make public art more accessible to BLV people.

Method

Participants

  • Any BLV person who is interested in public art exploration
  • Can also try to specifically recruit BLV artists for an additional perspective
  • Recruit some folks who have visited an art museum in the last year (to understand how things transfer)
  • Public art reaches more people, like people who wouldn’t otherwise be experiencing art by going to an art museum

Participant Screening Requirements

We are seeking blind and low vision participants for this study. We do not require participants to have a diagnosis.

Inclusion Criteria:

  • Identify as blind or low vision (self-identification is welcome)
  • Are 18+ years old

Exclusion Criteria:

  • Have a cognitive impairment which can impact their ability to give informed consent
  • Are not comfortable communicating in English

Part 1: Interview study with a co-design portion

Rationale: because we currently have no concrete understanding of what BLV people want for public art exploration (e.g., emulating museum visits vs. seeking another experience together), it would probably be valuable to first do an interview study to understand existing challenges or thoughts

Goals

  • Understand overarching preferences, challenges, concerns, etc.
  • Explore potential technologies / form factors participants would want and feel comfortable with
  • Explore potential features (e.g., automatic description, alerts for nearby art, etc.)
  • Think about the potential design space in advance to get some feedback on those dimensions but also have participants talk very “blue sky” about interesting design ideas

Potential interview questions — understanding current practices and thoughts about public art

  • Tell me about a recent art museum experience, if possible.
  • When you visit an art museum, what are your preferred methods for accessing art? Why?
  • Do you use any access tools when visiting a museum or consuming public art (e.g., Be My AI)?
  • What parts of artwork are most important for you to understand (e.g., the content, the visual style, etc.)?
  • Do you currently engage with art in non-museum contexts, such as murals, mosaics, or sculptures in the urban environment?
  • How would you describe your level of interest in engaging with public art?
  • If you were to explore urban art, how would you wish to engage? Why?
  • [if prompting is needed, or as a follow-up] What are your thoughts on existing strategies, such as those used for museum accessibility efforts, being applied to public art situations? Which do you think would be most effective and which would be least effective? Why?
  • Which devices would work well for supporting this? Which would not? Why?

Co-design could be done through verbal prototyping (similar to the 360° paper) or tactile engagement (if it’s the latter, would it need to be an in-person study?)

Questions

  • Have you used automated description tools to consume art, whether online, in a museum context, or public art? Explore prior experiences to help you understand why people are coming up with the ideas they have during co-design

Part 2: In-person prototype study

Rationale: based on our initial findings from the interviews / co-design, we can implement a prototype to actually test the ideas in practice because the experience of visiting and appreciating urban art is very distinct from museum experiences (which may be what participants are basing their responses on)

Goals

  • Assess the efficacy of the co-designed features
  • Elucidate additional considerations given the different environment

Prototype: likely will involve AI of some sort, given that the workshop paper brings that up and it’s one of the only ways to make this scalable I think?

Options for study

(which could also be decided based on participants’ interests from the interview)

  • A dedicated mural tour, similar to the Ithaca experience, where the focus is experiencing public art?
  • Or, a random walk around where art will be passed but it’s not a specific tour or art-focused pathing?

Interview Content

Introduction

Thank you for agreeing to participate in this study! My name is Lucy Jiang, and I am a PhD student researcher at the University of Washington. This project aims to understand how to make urban and public art, such as murals, mosaics, and sculptures, more accessible to blind and low vision people. Our team of graduate students and professors from Human Centered Design and Engineering and Computer Science are working together on this project.

This interview will consist of two main parts:
First, we’ll discuss your experiences, preferences, and challenges with engaging with urban artwork. Then, we'll discuss your previous interactions with artwork in museums and galleries. We will compare and contrast between your experiences in these two environments.
Then, we’ll explore potential designs, technologies, and features that could make urban and public art more accessible.

We estimate that the study will take around 90 minutes. Please remember that your participation in this study is voluntary and that you have the right to stop at any point. Upon completing the interview, you will be compensated $45 for your time and contributions.

At this time, I would like to ask for your permission to record this session. The recording will only be used for research purposes. Would you be okay with that?

Dimensions for Design Probes

Aspects about the art

  • Type (discrete): mural, mosaic, sculpture (motivated in the draft)
  • Purpose of art (discrete): monument, memorial, amenity, park, agora, pilgrimage (from the Public Art book), decoration
  • Scale (continuous): large, small
  • Reachability (discrete): can be reached or touched, cannot be reached or touched

Aspects about the experience

  • Goal / intention (continuous): seeking out a cultural or artistic experience (e.g., tourist attraction), walking by leisurely (e.g., streetscape), passing through on the way to somewhere else (e.g., transit center)
  • Density (continuous): crowded, isolated
  • Sociability (discrete): visiting by self, visiting with friend(s)

HCDE Research Showcase

Findings

Urban artwork poses distinct challenges for BLV people — at the intersection of art accessibility and urban navigation, neither of which fully accounts for how public art is discovered and engaged with in dynamic city spaces.

Discovery considerations: spontaneous notifications & artwork databases

Almost every participant (15 of 16) wanted location-aware notifications to alert them to nearby artwork. Sighted people turn a corner and discover a mural; BLV participants had no equivalent.

For spontaneous notifications, participants imagined proximity-based alerts that approximate the sense of surprise sighted people experience when encountering art by chance. They imagined auditory cues or alerts within about 10 feet of a piece, but were clear that safety came first: any audio from art descriptions could not interfere with navigation cues, traffic signals, or ambient awareness.

For artwork databases, pre-planning visits was described as invaluable for access. Participants wanted digital maps or searchable databases to learn about public art before or during a visit.

Description considerations: environmental integration & cultural significance

All 16 participants were at least somewhat positive about AI-generated descriptions. The ability to ask follow-up questions in real time was particularly valued — it let participants direct the interaction toward what they cared about, rather than receiving a generic overview.

But descriptions need to integrate environmental context and cultural significance, not just inventory visual elements. Accuracy errors hit differently with culturally significant art. One probe — an Indigenous American mosaic at a transit station — had its canoe misdescribed as a "rocket shape." When participants learned about the error, the response was visceral:

Participants were clear: for community-rooted public art, AI description errors don't just confuse — they risk cultural erasure.

Urban art is valued differently than museum art

Participants didn't just want the same museum accessibility tools transplanted outdoors. They valued urban art specifically because it was free, approachable, community-rooted, and serendipitous. One participant described encountering a fountain by accident: it was "kind of fun just to be there and listening to the water and the kids playing." That organic, spontaneous experience was exactly what was missing for BLV participants — and what any future technology needed to replicate, not replace.

Safety shapes everything

Twelve participants flagged safety as a primary constraint. Urban art exists in unpredictable environments — busy intersections, transit stairways, crowded markets. A mosaic on a transit station staircase during rush hour isn't a place anyone can stop to engage with art.

Multisensory access has real potential — and real constraints

All 16 participants wanted to use touch to engage with artwork independently. They appreciated that public art, unlike museum art, isn't "behind glass." But urban art created its own tactile barriers: murals can't be touched, sculptures are often too large or elevated to grasp fully, and some locations are unsafe to linger in. Participants suggested 3D scaled replicas, tactile maps, and haptic feedback for large murals — while also flagging practical concerns like weather damage, vandalism, and the cost of retrofitting existing infrastructure.

Fourteen participants supported spatial audio for navigation to artwork. Ten wanted music integrated into culturally significant pieces. Some suggested scent as a discovery mechanism. But across all of these: concern about disrupting others in shared public space.
