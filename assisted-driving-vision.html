Assisted Driving for Vision Challenges Co-Design Research

Role
UX Researcher
Institution
University of Washington
Focus
Driving · Visual impairment · Assistive technology
Methods
Co-design · Participant feedback

Problem Description

The problem is that navigating through traffic signs while driving can be difficult, especially when it is too bright outside, a lot of cars are driving around them, approaching cars use high beams during night, or with dry-eye syndrome.

Participant Needs

Participant Description: Our participant is facing visual disability - color weakness, astigmatism, and is experiencing vision loss from the dry eye problem recently. Specifically, he has suffered from the following dangerous and confusing situations during driving and biking:

Color weakness

Our participant has difficulty distinguishing between black and red colors under bright light and is unable to tell whether the red stopping traffic light is on or not.

Astigmatism

The participant also mentioned that his astigmatism makes him feel discomfort upon oncoming headlights and is unable to tell whether the high beam of the opposite car is on or not.

Dry eye

Vision loss caused by the dry eye problem has triggered the participant's anxiety about his existing driving and working routine.

Our participant needs a comfortable driving experience that doesn't compromise safety. This experience should accommodate his vision impairments by:

  • Providing clear visibility of traffic signals and lights,
  • Reducing glare and discomfort from oncoming headlights,
  • Offering features or aids that help compensate for vision loss due to dry eyes, especially during daily driving tasks.

Research goal

The goal of this research is to understand how people with difficulty seeing can read traffic signs and navigate road environments safely. Through co-design with Participant A, we sought to learn what support would relieve the issues faced by visually impaired drivers — and what constraints matter when assistive technology is considered.

Context

Drivers living with color-vision deficiency (CVD), astigmatism, or dry-eye syndrome face dangerous situations where visual challenges — difficult color distinction, glare, or blur — reduce reaction time and decision accuracy. Older drivers and all drivers under poor environmental conditions (weather, poor road maintenance) face overlapping challenges. The opportunity scales beyond a single participant to populations who share similar visual and environmental constraints on the road.

Color-vision-deficiency drivers

  • About 8% of males and 0.5% of females have some form of CVD, making it hard to distinguish red, yellow, and green traffic signals.
  • Field studies of professional drivers find measurable CVD prevalence and call for compensatory road-signal designs.

Astigmatism and glare-sensitive drivers

  • Uncorrected astigmatism significantly degrades night-driving performance; correcting it or adding clarity cues improves lane-keeping.

Drivers with dry-eye or screen-related visual fatigue

  • Dry-eye disease slows target recognition at intersections and roundabouts, increasing miss rates and reaction times.
  • Digital-eye-strain research shows glare and low contrast further worsen acuity.

Older drivers

  • Ageing eyes recover from glare more slowly and lose low-contrast sensitivity, increasing crash risk after dark.

All drivers in bad weather

  • In the past 10 years, 70% of weather-related crashes occur on wet pavement; rain alone accounts for nearly half of all weather crashes.

Summary

The session protocol consisted of an introduction, ice breaker, interview questions, design brainstorm, and conclusion. We first introduced ourselves and asked what everyone would do during the summer. Then, we asked questions like when the vision change happened and what their typical daily traveling routine is. Also, we asked how their visual disability affected their daily routines and driving or biking. Then, we asked if they have tried any assistive technologies and what kind of solution they would prefer. Lastly, we did a deeper dive into the possible design solution by asking about aspects of road and signs that make traveling easier or harder for them, what hardware improvements in a vehicle or bike that would help with their experience and their perspectives on self-driving cars.

Session 1: Interview Findings

Findings below synthesize Participant A’s responses from the Session 1 interview and design brainstorm.

Vision changes emerged recently and feel uncomfortable

Participant A first noticed vision changes in November of last year. Night driving has become uncomfortable, especially as it gets dark outside after 4pm. They described the changes as uncomfortable but not too bad — though they feel their vision is getting worse over time.

A short commute and heavy screen use shape daily life

Participant A’s daily travel routine is straightforward: drive to the office and back home, about 10 minutes each way. They do not bike. As a software engineer, they wake up using a smartphone, drive to work, and watch screens four to five hours a day for their job — plus another five to six hours on breaks, smartphone use, and video games, totaling roughly 10 hours of screen time daily. They noticed color weakness since elementary school.

Color weakness is mild in Seattle but surfaces in bright daylight

Participant A described color weakness as just a little bit of a challenge — green, yellow, and red are really different colors to them. Driving in Seattle, they don’t feel any challenge because signals are either red or green, and they can tell yellow and green apart and recognize red. The difficulty appears on sunny days when a signal may read as no light versus red: “Is that red or is there no light there?” It is not purely red but no light vs. red light during sunny days, not in a dark environment — in a dark environment they can tell it is black. They compared infrastructure in China, where traffic lights often have a small cover above the light so sunny light might not directly hit the red signal, though some traffic lights there do not have a cover. They have not developed a specific workaround beyond reasoning that if it is not green or yellow, it is red. They rated color weakness severity at 1 and said it is not a really annoying weakness and does not affect daily life.

Astigmatism and oncoming headlights are most severe at night

Compared to one or two years before, oncoming cars using high beams feel really annoying — Participant A feels like everyone is using high beam now. Their apartment has a garage with no natural light, and during sunny days sunlight around them makes them feel uncomfortable. They have glasses for driving but do not wear them daily; they can guess at license plates but cannot see the plate if a car is two cars’ distance away, though words are big enough that sometimes they cannot tell what the word is. Glasses are for short-sightedness and help a little but not really. Astigmatism has been happening for about half a year and they haven’t done anything yet beyond the driving glasses. They rated astigmatism at 1 during daytime and 4 during nighttime, and short sight at 1 or 2.

Dry-eye symptoms are recent, screen-linked, and hard to manage at work

Within one or two hours of playing games at night, Participant A’s eyes feel dry — something that did not happen one or two years before but has happened recently, and they feel like they need to take a rest. They described feeling “a little bit” anxious: their eye is not feeling comfortable and it might just start to damage their vision. When dry-eye symptoms flare, they use eye drops that make them feel a little bit better and take a rest for 10–20 minutes before returning to work. According to their doctor, the best move is to rest and watch greens or plants far away — go near the window and see stuff away from you. But their office has no window, and even when they want to rest there is nothing to see or view from their office. They leave the office and go outside for a walk; if busy, they might take the risk and finish the job first. They rated dry eye at 3 — it makes them feel uncomfortable.

No assistive tech tried; rest and eye exercises are the default

Participant A has not tried assistive technologies or services. In moments of difficulty, they do not turn to others for help — they take a rest, use eye drops, and close their eyes. They recalled eye exercises from school in China: as a Chinese student when you are young there is an eye exercise for 5 minutes, and they would do that kind of exercise and take a rest. They do not see it as a big problem because everyone has glasses and watches screens a lot; different tech like LCD and OLED can damage people’s eyes. They described LCD as blue light that is strong, and expressed skepticism that as tech develops it is aiming for making more money and “looking for different way to damage your eye.” On screen standards for their technical field, they wished manufacturers had clearer guidance: Samsung is more bright, LG is a little bit dim — if there were a standard for what screen is better for protecting vision, they would have preference and would use LG, since they need to use screens anyway and do not want to damage their eyes.

Physical solutions preferred; blue-light glasses and rest are current strategies

Participant A wears blue-light-blocking glasses while working to avoid more blue light, alongside eye drops and rest. They prefer a physical solution over a virtual one because virtual solutions require looking at a screen. For features that would work well, they imagined a virtual screen and machine that can help do eye exercise or improve vision, and physically a giant space to go outside for hiking and green or other treatment. Physical is better, but some virtual exercise could help. They did not rank alerts or feedback methods or priority situations in the interview.

Design brainstorm surfaced voice cues, existing ADAS, and openness to self-driving

Participant A drives a Subaru Crosstrek. They said software does not help for their eye problem, but they do not have to watch traffic lights if a system announces when the light turns green — a feature they know Tesla has with a voice or beep — and that would help other people who look at their phones as well. It would be helpful if the car could tell them when there is a car in front. For oncoming headlamps, only approaching cars are the issue; when a car is behind them, their Subaru dims light a little bit. Their Subaru’s Eyesight feature and other cars’ hard-stop when something is in front really help. They use Android Auto with a Samsung phone every time they drive but find it limited — Google Assistant uses Chinese, localization is a problem, and they want more voice control for navigation (e.g., “go back to home” or “go to office”). They noted two cameras in front do not have the ability to view the traffic light, so smartphone or software needs hardware support. On full self-driving: yes in an ideal environment the software will make less of a problem than a human, but the road is very complicated — if they are driving it is their responsibility, but if the car is driving, who takes responsibility? They would like a totally fully self-driving car and would pay someone to drive if eyesight became 10 times worse; self-driving is the same situation, with responsibility as the major concern.

Session 2: Co-design Findings

Findings below synthesize Participant A’s feedback on team brainstorm concepts and the direction chosen at the end of the co-design session.

Traffic signal: voice alerts beat dashboard devices; projection preferred

On the dashboard camera device, Participant A said it looks fine but questioned how much space it would take on the dashboard — if focused on the dashboard, would it be dangerous because they are paying attention away from the road? If there is a car in front, would it get in the way? “Just the voice alert is enough.” A voice cue for yellow would also help people who aren’t paying attention. They wanted a countdown, wished the display were higher at the window, and said projection is a good idea. On the smart windshield overlay, they noted that during rainy days or night time it is hard to tell if there is a line there — especially white lines and how many white lines are on the road — but it is not a huge issue.

Glare: smart glasses are easy; windshield overlay avoids another wearable

Participant A said smart glasses would help in some way because wearing glasses is an easy solution compared to the window idea, though they are scared how dark it would be — if it would be too dark. They called the design good. For high beams from behind, the rear view mirror can hit the side of their eye so it is not very annoying, but stopping it would help. On the smart windshield overlay, they said “That is definitely better bc then I do not need to wear glasses.”

Dry eye: break reminders help; color detection and privacy matter more

On blink-tracking smart glasses, Participant A said telling them when to take breaks will be helpful and they would prefer the glasses — but detecting color change is better. They raised data privacy: if there is a lighter simpler sensor instead of camera for higher data privacy. They did not offer feedback on the in-car eye hydration system or the windshield overlay variant for this task.

Key insights: AR over another screen; minimal audio on routine commutes

Across tasks, Participant A said for their personal interest AR is better but glasses is a more achievable product. They do not want another screen on the car — though AR is not a brand new screen, and the large pad in Tesla is too big. Data privacy remained a concern. For AR specifically: on rainy days, if AR can point out “here is the line” and highlight it that would be better; highlight the traffic light and help detect cars or approaching people. On audio: if they are just going to work, they don’t want any audio — they just want music. They don’t want an agent jumping in and slowing them down.

Design direction: AR

The session concluded with a concrete design direction: AR. Participant A’s preferences — integrated overlays rather than dashboard devices, windshield-based glare reduction over additional wearables, voice-only signal cues, and privacy-conscious sensors — shaped this choice as the most promising direction to refine in follow-up sessions.

Co-design sessions

We invited Participant A to a series of co-design sessions on Zoom. Early sessions used low-fidelity sketches shared through FigJam so the participant could react to visuals rather than imagine concepts from verbal description alone. In the final 15-minute session, we shared prototype drawings through the chat feature, asked for initial feedback, and followed up with clarifying questions to deepen our understanding of what support would matter for safe driving.

Follow-up prototype findings

Findings below synthesize Participant A’s feedback across later co-design sessions — from AR concept refinement through final prototype review.

Traffic-signal information belongs in the primary field of view

Participant A valued traffic-signal cues that deliver useful information without occupying much space or requiring them to look at a separate device — which they felt could be distracting. During an earlier session, they raised similar concerns about a dashboard-mounted camera: its placement felt potentially distracting, and they worried it might fail to detect a signal when a tall vehicle blocked the view ahead. Information integrated into the driving view, rather than routed through a secondary screen, aligned better with how they wanted to stay oriented on the road.

Countdown timing carries a safety trade-off

Participant A found a textual countdown for signal changes useful for preparing to stop or go. At the same time, they expressed concern that a visible timer might encourage drivers to speed up in an attempt to beat the light — rather than slowing down as they might without a countdown — potentially leading to dangerous situations at intersections. The feedback surfaced a tension between clarity and behavior: timing cues that feel helpful to one driver may introduce risk if they nudge others toward rushing.

Lane guidance matters most when visibility drops

During a later session, Participant A described difficulty identifying and differentiating lanes during rainy nights — a low-vision issue that has developed over several years and contributes to fear and reduced confidence while driving. They appreciated lane guidance paired with signal-distance cues, as long as the information is accurate. They also wanted the ability to adjust the transparency of lane indicators, reflecting a broader preference for controlling how much visual augmentation appears on screen rather than accepting fixed defaults.

Glare reduction is welcome — with user control

Participant A appreciated approaches that reduce glare from oncoming headlights, which their astigmatism makes especially uncomfortable. Similar to lane guidance, they wanted control over the level of dimming applied — not a single preset. The preference for adjustable opacity and dimming appeared consistently across features: Participant A wanted assistive overlays to feel supportive without becoming visually overwhelming or opaque.

Wearables are a non-starter for this driver

Participant A does not prefer wearable devices. They already wear glasses and were reluctant to add another wearable layer — whether smart glasses for glare reduction or blink sensors for dry-eye monitoring. Non-wearable, in-vehicle approaches aligned better with their existing routine and comfort. This constraint shaped which concepts received further discussion and which were set aside early in co-design.

Dry-eye monitoring overlaps with existing vehicle alerts

Participant A found dry-eye monitoring somewhat redundant because their car already includes a similar driver-fatigue alert. They noted it would not be particularly useful on most days, since they typically drive under normal conditions. The feedback highlighted that assistive features perceived as duplicating capabilities already built into the vehicle may not feel worth the added complexity — even when the underlying symptom (vision loss from dry eye) remains a real concern.

Bright-day signal readability was an unanticipated pain point

One of the biggest surprises from co-design was learning that traffic signals without sun-shade visors can be hard to read on bright, sunny days — an issue the research team had not considered before the session. Once Participant A pointed it out, it made immediate sense and likely affects many more drivers than color weakness alone. The discovery extended the research scope beyond night driving and glare toward environmental conditions that wash out signal visibility during daylight hours.

Personalization emerged as a cross-cutting need

Across sessions, Participant A consistently preferred control over feature parameters — transparency of lane overlays, dimming intensity, and the ability to enable or disable individual aids — rather than fixed settings imposed on every driver. Countdown timing, lane guidance, and glare reduction each received positive reactions when described as adjustable. The pattern suggests that drivers managing multiple vision impairments may need fine-grained tuning to match daily conditions, comfort thresholds, and what their vehicle already provides.
