Nordstrom Rack “Buy Online & Pick Up In Store” Research

Team
Shay Duan, Khadijah Ingawa, Tess Matthews, Amy Lee
Client
Nordstrom
Focus
Same-day pickup · Nordstrom Rack desktop website
Methods
Moderated usability testing · Think Aloud protocol
Nordstromrack.com ↗

Background

This usability study focuses on the user flow of purchasing same-day pickup items on the Nordstrom Rack desktop website. Same-day pick-up lets customers reserve products online while driving in-store visits, which can lead to additional purchases.

Angela, a Principal UX researcher at Nordstrom, already had this problem in mind because she one day saw that the same- or next-day pickup item filter was not working as expected. Also, this topic was not tested yet with real online shoppers. So she needed help with the next step of defining the testing parameters, recruiting users, facilitating tests, and reporting the results to her UX team.

The goal of the study was to identify pain points for customers attempting to add same-day pickup items to their shopping bag and initiate checkout—including navigating pickup from multiple store locations, whether shoppers feel comfortable proceeding when pickup logistics may not be clear pre-checkout, and how easily they can find in-store pickup information. The scope was 8 remote usability tests with online shoppers who live within driving distance of a Nordstrom Rack store. So, the core research question she needed help in answering was: “how easily and successfully can users navigate the Nordstrom Rack desktop website to find and purchase same or next-day pickup items?”

Method and Reasoning

We chose moderated usability testing because it allows for real-time observation and exploration of user behavior. Through usability testing, we wanted to capture participants’ thought processes using the Think Aloud protocol as they navigate purchasing pickup items on the Nordstrom Rack website—and surface their expectations and decision-making in ways a survey would not. Moderators could notice confusion, ask follow-ups, and adapt when unexpected issues came up, so we could collect both qualitative and quantitative insight to inform the design.

We ran the study remotely with 8 online shoppers who live within driving distance of a Nordstrom Rack store. We created a screener survey to gather participants. Running remotely meant people could use their own laptops the way they would actually shop on the site, and our team could watch and record their screens over Zoom.

Participants had to live within driving distance of a Nordstrom Rack store and shop online for clothing, makeup, or home goods at least three times per year. We required the first because same-day pickup only works if you can actually get to a store, so we wanted people who could really use the feature. We required the second so they would already be comfortable shopping online, and we could focus on how they experienced the pickup flow. We also screened out researchers and retail workers to reduce bias, and aimed for a mix of demographics and pickup experience—including, if possible, someone who had purchased a pickup item in the past six months.

In each usability test, we first asked about their own experiences with online shopping. Then we gave them three scenarios with tasks and encouraged them to think aloud. Each scenario had its own goal.

The first was whether they could successfully find a pickup item and start checkout—the basic path for any pickup shopper—and whether people understood how pickup items differ from other options like shipping.

The second was whether buying pickup items from different stores at the same time went smoothly. The item someone wants is not always at the store nearest them, so we wanted to see how easy it is to switch stores and buy from more than one. We also noticed some confusing behavior when you do that: at checkout, the site shows a “sold out” message for items that are actually available at another store, and items already in the bag can be reassigned to the new store without a clear notice. We wanted to see how people reacted to that and how the messaging could be clearer.

The third was how easily they could find information about pickup before buying. Pickup details are not always clear before checkout, so we wanted to see whether people could find them easily.

Favorite Research Moment

I wanted to share my favorite research moment from the remote usability testing, because it showed us how hard it is to find a same-day pickup item on the website.

Before we ran the usability tests, we tried the filters ourselves. One of our teammates applied the “Available Today” filter and a size filter. But when she clicked on the first search result, none of the colors were available in that size.

This was my favorite moment because it showed how difficult it was to find the same-day pickup item that was truly available for same-day pickup. And we found that before the usability testing, which told us this was worth testing.

Findings

Here is a little bit about the findings from the usability tests. The pickup experience broke down in a few places.

First, the “Available Today” filter did not work as expected. · Severity: High

Participants expected every result to be available for same-day pickup. The filter operates at the product level, not the size level, so products showed up as long as at least one size or color was available for pickup — even if the shopper’s selected size was entirely unavailable. People clicked through about 10.6 size and color combinations on average to find a pair of shoes they could actually pick up. When “Available Today” and a size filter were applied together, some results still appeared with no colors available in that size for same- or next-day pickup — a direct contradiction of what the filter implies. Some participants almost gave up; 3 of 8 tried to abandon the task 1–3 times because the filters felt broken. (7 of 8 applied “Available Today”; 4 of 8 also applied a size filter. Separately, 2 of 8 never noticed the filter at all — one gave up, another got lucky on the first try.)

Then, on the product page, fulfillment silently switched from pickup to ship to store. · Severity: High

Selecting a size or color that wasn’t available for same-day pickup changed “Free pickup” to “Free ship to store.” That UI change is subtle and easy to miss when someone is focused on picking a size. Some participants didn’t notice and added items to their bag thinking they could get them by tomorrow. 4 of 8 added a “ship to store” item believing it was available for same-day pickup. One participant accidentally added a ship-to-store item after assuming “Available Today” meant everything would be ready for pickup by today or tomorrow — then realized it only meant the item was purchasable today, and that there didn’t seem to be a filter for what they actually wanted.

Also, buying from more than one store broke down at checkout. · Severity: Medium

When participants added pickup items from Store A, switched location, then added items from Store B, everything consolidated under Store B at checkout — without a clear, proactive notice. If Store A items weren’t available for same-day pickup at Store B, fulfillment changed quietly. A “sold out” message also appeared for items that were still available elsewhere: one item was moved to “Saved for Later” with “We moved your sold-out items to Saved for Later,” even though a popup mentioned it was “available nearby.” That “sold-out” label made people unsure whether the item was globally out of stock or just unavailable at the selected store. 5 of 8 expected to check out from multiple stores in one purchase; all 8 were confused or annoyed when “Available Today” items became unavailable at checkout. 5 of 8 noticed the store switch on their own; 3 of 8 needed a prompt.

When both items were available at Store B, consolidation was actually well-received — people liked not making multiple trips. The problem was the silent fulfillment downgrade and the misleading sold-out language.

Finally, pickup details were hard to find. · Severity: Low (logistics discoverability)

Information on when orders are ready for pickup and how long they are held is absent from the checkout page, product details page, and main FAQ page. Most participants first looked on the checkout or bag page (6 of 8), and it took an average of 7 clicks to find how long an order would be held. One person gave up and turned to Google.

So the pickup experience broke down when what the site showed did not match what was actually available.

Data Analysis Process

After each remote session, we had the Zoom recording of the participant’s screen and audio, the notes our notetaker took during the test, and a shared Google Sheet where we logged success or failure on every task.

We started with the quantitative side. From that sheet we calculated task success — 82% overall. That told us the Nordstrom Rack site worked for most people on the pickup path, and it highlighted which tasks people failed so we knew where to dig first.

Then we went back through what participants did and said, task by task — where they hesitated, what they misread, and why they got stuck — and grouped those moments into themes. For each theme we assigned a severity rating using the same scale the Nordstrom UX team uses: higher when a problem blocked completing a task, middle when it caused significant delay or frustration, and lower when the impact on the experience was minor.

We backed each finding with a labeled participant quote or example so Angela’s team could see the evidence behind every recommendation, then wrote a recommendation for each problem.

Design Recommendations

I’ll walk through our design recommendations in the order shoppers move through the pickup experience — find, choose, check out, then understand what happens next.

First was finding the item. The “Available Today” filter surfaced products that weren’t really available in the shopper’s size, so we recommended reviewing the filter logic — especially when it’s combined with a size filter. If someone filters for “Available Today” and a size, every result should have at least one color in that size that can be picked up today. On the product page, unavailable sizes and colors could be greyed out with an “Available on” date, so people don’t have to click through option after option to find one they can actually pick up.

Second was choosing it. Shoppers often missed when “Free pickup” quietly changed to “Free ship to store,” so we recommended making those two labels more visually distinct before the item goes into the bag. If the difference is clear in that moment, shoppers won’t assume something will be ready tomorrow when it won’t.

Third was checking out. The site changed store assignment and fulfillment without clearly telling people, so we recommended making those changes visible. When someone switches stores with pickup items already in the bag, a warning could say what will change — for example, “Changing your store will update the pickup location for all items in your bag.” At checkout, a notification could confirm when items were reassigned to a different store. And because the “sold out” language was confusing for items still available nearby, we recommended rewording it to say what actually happened — such as “We moved your items available at other stores to Saved for Later” — so shoppers know the item is still available, just not at this store.

Fourth was knowing what happens next. Pickup timing and hold policy were missing from the places people looked first, so we recommended adding when the order is ready and how long it’s held to the checkout page, the shopping bag, or the FAQ — wherever shoppers already expect that information.

Impact

This study gave us a clear picture of how easily — and how successfully — shoppers can navigate the Nordstrom Rack desktop site to find and purchase same- or next-day pickup items. Through eight remote usability tests we surfaced where the pickup experience breaks down, then turned those moments into findings, severity-rated themes, and design recommendations for Angela’s UX team.

We presented the work to Nordstrom. Angela noted that our group stayed organized and proactive throughout the project — from defining the testing parameters and recruiting through facilitating sessions and reporting back.

Since then, changes that match our recommendations have gone live on nordstromrack.com. The “Available Today” filter works more reliably, and pickup information is easier to find where shoppers look for it.
